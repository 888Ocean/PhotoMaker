# Littoral Diffraction

Littoral Diffraction begins with the camera's imperfect encounter with light. The sea is not treated as a backdrop but as a dark optical field in which a handful of reflections flare, split, and briefly become stars. The effect is selective: most of the image remains quiet so each burst feels discovered rather than applied.

Each frame keeps the photograph full-bleed and materially present. Cropping follows the natural horizon and the human figures, while the starbursts trace a visual path through the water. No panel, border, or explanatory block interrupts the image. The composition should feel like a found fragment from a personal archive, meticulously edited but never over-designed.

The palette is built from the source light: marine blue, oxidized teal, wet sand, and a restrained spectral glint. The diffraction rays carry the faintest chromatic separation—cyan, rose, and warm gold—only at their edges. Color calibration is painstaking and subtle, preserving the camera's atmosphere while making the brightest points feel almost tactile.

Across the sequence, scale shifts from infrastructure to people to open water. The wind turbines provide a measured horizontal rhythm; the kayaks provide a small, warm counter-movement; the glittering surface becomes the connective tissue. Repetition is felt through the recurring horizon and changing star positions, not through graphic decoration.

Words are reduced to small, quiet traces: a date, a frame number, a two-word observation. Typography sits inside the photographic space like a note written after the fact. Every flare, crop, and letter is the product of deep expertise and master-level restraint, as if the work had been refined over countless hours until nothing remained that did not belong.
